# Introduction: establish the problem and contribution

Use this reference only when writing or substantially restructuring an Introduction. Its job is to make the research problem and the paper's reason for existing intelligible to the intended audience. It is not required to follow a fixed number of paragraphs or a universal funnel structure.

## Move from shared context to the research gap

Start from a premise the intended reader can recognize and that is genuinely relevant to the paper. Introduce the issue, unresolved limitation, or contrast that leads to the research question. **Prefer a progression from established context to new information**: each claim should prepare the reader for the next step toward the gap. Cite prior work where needed to substantiate the background, not merely to satisfy an opening-sentence citation pattern.

An opening that could introduce dozens of unrelated papers may be too generic if the next sentences do not narrow it toward this specific problem. But a general or cited opening is not inherently wrong. Ask whether readers can see why this premise is the right starting point and how it leads toward the gap.

## Distinguish gap, question, approach, and contribution

The gap should identify a meaningful limitation or unanswered question in the available literature, without inventing a claim that no prior work exists. Explain why resolving the gap matters. State the question or objective precisely enough for the reader to recognize what is studied and what is outside scope. Present the proposed approach or completed finding at a level appropriate for the Introduction, and express the contribution in terms of what the paper establishes or enables—not unsupported words such as “novel” or “first.”

Claims of novelty, practical importance, or state-of-the-art performance need appropriate reference points or evidence. If the supplied material cannot establish them, use a bounded claim or flag the missing basis instead of supplying invented citations or comparisons. For pre-experiment manuscripts, distinguish hypotheses and proposed evaluations from demonstrated findings; for post-experiment manuscripts, match the contribution to actual results.

## Check the reader's route into the paper

Read the Introduction as a connected explanation: Can the reader recover the path from the opening premise to the research gap, then from the gap to the question and contribution? Do transitions express real causal, contrastive, or evidential relationships rather than simply using “Furthermore” or “Therefore”? Is background detail sufficient but not distracting? Does the Introduction promise only what the rest of the manuscript can deliver?

Use paragraph writing to give each part an intelligible role, but do not force a one-paragraph-per-function template. An overview figure or example can be useful if it clarifies the central problem or comparison; it is not obligatory.
