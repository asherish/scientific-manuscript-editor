# Introduction: establish the problem and contribution

The Introduction explains **why the research problem matters and why this paper is needed**. By the end, readers should understand the route from established knowledge to the unresolved question, the proposed approach, and the contribution the paper can support. 

## Move from shared context to the research gap

The Introduction **should** start from a premise the intended reader can recognize and that is genuinely relevant to the paper. Introduce the issue, unresolved limitation, or contrast that leads to the research question. **Prefer a progression from established context to new information**: each claim should prepare the reader for the next step toward the gap. Cite prior work where needed to substantiate the background, not merely to satisfy an opening-sentence citation pattern.

An opening that could introduce dozens of unrelated papers may be too generic if the next sentences do not narrow it toward this specific problem. But a general or cited opening is not inherently wrong. Ask whether readers can see why this premise is the right starting point and how it leads toward the gap.

### Use CARS as a planning lens

Swales's **Create a Research Space (CARS)** model distinguishes three communicative moves: establish the research territory, identify a niche or unresolved problem, and occupy that niche by stating what this study addresses or establishes. It helps test whether the Introduction connects established knowledge, the remaining gap, and this paper's contribution. 

A useful variant is a progressively narrowing gap: a broad scientific limitation, a more specific unresolved aspect, and the particular question addressed here. The final passage then explains how the paper approaches that question and, when results exist, what it found. **Consider** this narrower progression when the research genuinely has several levels of unresolved questions.

When designing the progression, make each move prepare the next: establish the relevant phenomenon, narrow to what is known, identify the specific unresolved issue, explain why it matters, and show how the present study addresses it. Use reader questions to test for missing links.

Ground claims that the problem is difficult in identifiable constraints, representative failure cases, unresolved mechanisms, or evidence from relevant studies. A concrete use case may help readers understand why the task or gap matters, but illustrative cases do not demonstrate that all existing approaches fail. Distinguish a broad motivation from the precise target problem addressed by this study.

## Distinguish gap, question, approach, and contribution

The gap should identify a meaningful limitation or unanswered question in the available literature, without inventing a claim that no prior work exists. Explain why resolving the gap matters. State the question or objective precisely enough for the reader to recognize what is studied and what is outside scope. Present the proposed approach or completed finding at a level appropriate for the Introduction, and express the contribution in terms of what the paper establishes or enables—not unsupported words such as “novel” or “first.”

Claims of novelty, practical importance, or state-of-the-art performance need appropriate reference points or evidence. If the supplied material cannot establish them, use a bounded claim or flag the missing basis instead of supplying invented citations or comparisons. For pre-experiment manuscripts, distinguish hypotheses and proposed evaluations from demonstrated findings; for post-experiment manuscripts, match the contribution to actual results.

Prioritize the most consequential distinction from the closest prior approaches, and explain the mechanism or reasoning by which that choice may address the gap. A proposed rationale is not evidence of successful performance. Present the paper's substantive contributions at the level the underlying work can support, while keeping the emphasis on the distinctions relevant to this research. When a figure would clarify the research setting, central idea, or comparison, **consider** an overview figure.

## Make contributions and early evidence navigable

When a paper makes several consequential claims, consider short, descriptive labels that remain recognizable from the Abstract through the experimental questions, Results, and Conclusion. Align each claim with evidence or a planned evaluation, and preserve conditions that limit it. The labels should aid navigation across sections.

For completed research, consider an early **crown-jewel figure** when a legible, representative result or real-world example would help readers grasp the central contribution. A problem-setting example or conceptual overview may be more useful for a method-heavy or pre-experiment paper. Choose a figure for what it genuinely establishes; never select only favorable evidence to disguise variation or limitations.  Consult `figures.md` for design and caption details.

## Check the reader's route into the paper

Read the Introduction as a connected explanation: Can the reader recover the path from the opening premise to the research gap, then from the gap to the question and contribution? Do transitions express real causal, contrastive, or evidential relationships rather than simply using “Furthermore” or “Therefore”? Is background detail sufficient but not distracting? Does the Introduction promise only what the rest of the manuscript can deliver?

Use paragraph writing to give each part an intelligible role, and let paragraph boundaries follow meaningful explanatory units. Any representative example or overview figure discussed above should support that progression rather than substitute for the explanation.

## Sources and applicability

- Swales (1990), *Genre Analysis: English in Academic and Research Settings* — the original CARS model.
- Mensh and Kording (2017), *Ten simple rules for structuring papers* — progression from broad context to a paper-specific gap: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619
- MIT EECS Communication Lab, *Paper: Introduction* — problem, motivation, solution, and contribution as reader questions: https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/journal-article-introduction/
- Faloutsos, *MetaPaper v14* — optional early, representative evidence figure: https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~christos/MetaPaper/faloutsos_MetaPaper_v14.pdf

These models are diagnostic aids; scientific grounding and the target venue remain decisive. Adapt attention-routing advice to the research and venue: a selective showcase is not a substitute for balanced Results.
